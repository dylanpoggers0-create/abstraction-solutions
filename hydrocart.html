What HydroCart is

HydroCart is our showpiece, not a product for sale. It holds six plants in a small footprint and runs a nearly autonomous loop: sensors measure the system, our own AI model learns from those readings, and it predicts where each reading is heading so problems show up before they hurt the plants.

The same approach works for other equipment that needs watching, which is why we use it to show clients what a custom AI system can do.

How the AI loop works

  1. Measure. An Arduino Mega reads pH, the water level in two tanks, and the temperature and humidity at two points on the cart.
  2. Remember. It saves 10-minute averages on the board itself, enough for about 2.6 days of readings even with no computer attached.
  3. Forecast. A Python app runs our AI model and predicts every reading 30 minutes, 6 hours and 24 hours ahead.
  4. Check itself. Each forecast is scored against five simpler methods using rules we set before looking at the results, so we only trust the AI where it actually does better.
  5. Show it. Live readings and forecasts appear on a dashboard, so the few hands-on steps happen at the right time instead of on a guess.

What's on the cart

  • A rolling cart with a fixed central tower holding six plants
  • A tote reservoir at the bottom, plus a backup tank up top
  • A small pump that carries the nutrient solution up the tower so it trickles back down past the roots
  • An aerator and a heater for the water
  • Grow lights
  • An Arduino Mega with pH, water-level, temperature and humidity sensors

Most of the cart is built from reused parts, with about $50 in new parts.

Why grow without soil

Hydroponics is a way to grow plants without soil. A pH-adjusted water solution carries nutrients straight to the roots, sometimes with an inert medium such as coco coir or rockwool for support. That is the method behind HydroCart.

You can grow indoors year-round, including in winter, because the plants are not tied to field weather (University of Missouri Extension, 2024). Vertical towers also fit in a small indoor footprint, which is useful in a city where outdoor growing space is limited.

Closed or recirculating systems can use water efficiently. In one peer-reviewed comparison of lettuce grown in Yuma, Arizona, hydroponic greenhouse production was estimated at about 20 liters of water per kilogram of lettuce per year, compared with about 250 liters for conventional field lettuce — roughly 13 times less water per kilogram, or about 92% less (Barbosa et al., 2015). Those figures are for that crop and setting, not a promise for every plant in every tower.

The same study found much higher yield per square meter for that hydroponic lettuce, and also much higher energy use per kilogram than field production. Hydroponics is not automatically “low energy,” so we do not claim it is.

Typical hydroponic crops include leafy greens, herbs, tomatoes, peppers, and strawberries. NASA has also tested hydroponic and aeroponic growing without soil on the International Space Station (NASA XROOTS).

What is hydroponics?

Growing plants without soil, with nutrients delivered in water.

Can you grow year-round?

Indoor hydroponic crops can be grown year-round, including in winter, according to University of Missouri Extension.

Does it use less water?

Recirculating hydroponic systems can use much less water per kilogram than field growing; a 2015 lettuce study in Arizona estimated about 92% less water per kilogram. Results vary by crop and setup.

Does it use less electricity?

Not necessarily. That same study found much higher energy use per kilogram than field lettuce. We will list HydroCart's measured draw here if we publish one.

Is the food safe to eat?

Yes, hydroponically grown food is eaten routinely. Indoor growing can reduce contact with wildlife that sometimes carry foodborne pathogens; you still need clean handling and a clean nutrient solution.
